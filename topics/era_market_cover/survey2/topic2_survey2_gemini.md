# **Quantitative Evaluation of Era-Targeted Cover Generation and Stem-Wise Analysis**

The evaluation of generative music and the attribution of latent stylistic properties within neural representations represent two of the most complex challenges in contemporary Music Information Retrieval (MIR). When analyzing the specific case of era-targeted cover generation—where a system must preserve the melodic, harmonic, and vocal identity of a source track while imposing the stylistic and chronologically specific acoustic properties of a target decade—the evaluation paradigm must strictly isolate content preservation from stylistic translation. Compounding this challenge is the constraint of extremely small evaluation sets. Standard evaluation protocols in deep learning require thousands of samples to achieve statistical power; however, evaluating generative pipelines often involves highly curated, small-scale datasets where computational expense and manual curation limit the sample size to tens of tracks.  
This report provides an exhaustive methodological framework for the quantitative evaluation of era-targeted cover generation and stem-wise temporal analysis. It addresses the fundamental instability of linear probing under domain shift, proposes advanced metrics for content preservation and audio quality that bypass small-sample biases, and establishes a rigorous statistical pipeline utilizing linear mixed-effects models to extract valid inferences from highly constrained datasets.

## **1\. Era and Style Presence in Generated Music**

Quantifying whether a generated audio clip successfully embodies the acoustic and stylistic properties of a target era requires metrics that can operate independently of the track's melodic content. The objective is to computationally verify the presence of an era's signature production techniques, instrumentation, and harmonic vocabulary.

### **Classifier-Based Scores and the Domain-Shift Vulnerability**

The most direct approach to era validation utilizes pre-trained music-tagging models. Models such as the Essentia Discogs-EffNet utilize an EfficientNet-B0 architecture trained on editorial metadata to predict multi-label stylistic activations across hundreds of Discogs categories1. While these classifiers demonstrate high accuracy on real-world audio, they are uniquely vulnerable to domain shift when applied to generated audio. Generative audio models, particularly those reliant on discrete neural audio codecs like EnCodec or SoundStream, imprint distinct structural artifacts onto the output waveform3. The transposed convolution layers within codec decoders introduce periodic spectral peaks and high-frequency phase distortions, fundamentally altering the acoustic manifold6.  
Because conventional classifiers learn to rely on the natural spectral roll-off and noise floor characteristics of analog and early digital recordings, the sudden presence of neural codec artifacts causes catastrophic distribution shifts. The classifier interprets the codec artifacts as an overriding "modern generative" signature, routinely classifying all outputs as post-2000s electronic music regardless of the underlying compositional style6. Consequently, raw classifier probabilities cannot be trusted as an absolute metric for era presence in generated audio.

### **Embedding-Distance Metrics**

To circumvent the brittleness of absolute classification, distribution-based metrics quantify the similarity between the generated audio and a reference corpus of genuine era-specific recordings in a latent embedding space.  
The Fréchet Audio Distance (FAD) represents the standard metric for generative audio evaluation. FAD extracts embeddings using models such as VGGish, PANNs, or CLAP, and fits a multivariate Gaussian distribution to both the reference and generated sets9. The Fréchet distance between these distributions provides a reference-free approximation of acoustic similarity. However, FAD relies heavily on the assumption that the underlying embeddings are normally distributed, and more critically, it requires massive sample sizes to reliably estimate the covariance matrices. When the sample size drops to constraints such as twelve songs, the sample covariance matrix becomes mathematically singular or highly biased, resulting in FAD scores that reflect sample size artifacts rather than perceptual quality10. The FAD\-![][image1] extrapolation technique mitigates this by estimating the asymptotic score as the sample size approaches infinity, but it still struggles with extreme data scarcity12.  
The Kernel Audio Distance (KAD) offers a mathematically superior alternative for small datasets. KAD utilizes the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) computed over a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) using a Gaussian kernel14. Unlike FAD, KAD is distribution-free; it does not assume Gaussianity and computes distance by comparing the empirical kernel mean embeddings of the two datasets. Crucially, the MMD provides an unbiased finite-sample estimator16. This allows KAD to remain highly stable even when evaluating sets comprising mere dozens of tracks, making it the optimal distributional metric for highly constrained experimental designs16.  
Additionally, per-song distance metrics extract the embedding of a single generated track and compute its spatial distance to the centroid of the reference manifold. This approach enables granular outlier detection, isolating specific seeds or tracks where the generative model suffered total stylistic failure9.

### **Text-Audio Alignment and CLAP Scores**

For text-conditioned generation, the Contrastive Language-Audio Pretraining (CLAP) score computes the cosine similarity between the generated audio embedding and the conditioning text prompt (e.g., "1960s pop rock")11. While CLAP is ubiquitous in general text-to-audio evaluation, its precision regarding historical nuance is questionable. CLAP embeddings are typically optimized for broad semantic alignment rather than fine-grained temporal chronologies21. Furthermore, optimizing generative hyperparameters explicitly for CLAP scores introduces the risk of reward hacking, where the model sacrifices acoustic fidelity or structural coherence to force extreme semantic alignment23.

### **Production-Feature Metrics**

A highly robust, deterministic approach involves extracting low-level physical and statistical production features utilizing libraries such as Essentia or Librosa26. Attributes including spectral tilt, dynamic range, integrated loudness (measured in LUFS via EBU R128 standards), and high-frequency roll-off exhibit thoroughly documented chronological trends27. For example, comparing the crest factor and integrated loudness provides a direct quantification of mastering styles, easily separating the high-dynamic-range recordings of the 1970s from the heavily limited outputs of the early 2000s loudness war. Because these metrics are mathematical properties of the signal, they require no reference manifolds and suffer zero bias at small sample sizes.

## **2\. Resolving the Domain-Shift Problem**

The vulnerability of linear probes and distance metrics to generative artifacts necessitates interventions that restore metric validity. The neural audio codec bottleneck homogenizes the high-frequency spectrum, causing real reference audio and generated audio to occupy fundamentally different acoustic domains7. To ensure that a probe or distance metric measures era-specific stylistic transfer rather than merely detecting codec artifacts, the evaluation pipeline must enforce domain parity.

### **Codec-Resynthesized Anchoring**

The most rigorous and established methodology for correcting codec-induced domain shift is forward-pass resynthesis. Rather than comparing the generated audio to pristine studio references, the ground-truth era recordings are passed through the exact same frozen neural codec used by the generative model (e.g., EnCodec)28. This process intentionally degrades the reference audio, implanting the identical quantization noise, phase distortion, and spectral artifacts present in the generated output. Consequently, the real and generated audio distributions are forced into the same artifact domain. When an era classifier or KAD metric is subsequently computed against these resynthesized references, the metric is forced to ignore the shared high-frequency codec artifacts and instead evaluate the structural, harmonic, and temporal variance that actually defines the musical era.

### **Difference-in-Differences (Relative Scoring)**

An alternative calibration technique leverages a control baseline generated via a neutral text prompt. The generative model is tasked with processing the input song using a purely descriptive, non-temporal prompt (e.g., "A standard music track"). The linear probe or KAD metric evaluates this neutral generation to establish a baseline domain-shift penalty. Subsequently, the era-targeted generation is evaluated. The absolute score is discarded in favor of the relative shift (the difference-in-differences). If the targeted prompt successfully imparts 1980s stylistic characteristics, the relative distance to the 1980s reference manifold will shrink significantly compared to the neutral baseline. This relative measurement subtracts the static artifact bias, isolating the causal effect of the era condition.

### **Counterfactual DSP Controls**

A custom, deterministic control involves applying aggressive, destructive digital signal processing to both the reference and generated audio prior to embedding extraction. A processing chain consisting of a steep low-pass filter (cutoff at the known effective bandwidth of the codec, roughly 200–300 Hz for certain residual artifacts6), coupled with dynamic range saturation and vinyl noise, effectively destroys the pristine high-frequency characteristics of the real audio while simultaneously masking the codec artifacts of the generated audio. This forces downstream embeddings like MERT or MuQ to rely exclusively on low- and mid-band structural and harmonic arrangements rather than high-frequency production polish29.  
While training a dedicated linear probe directly on synthetically generated era-labeled audio is technically feasible, it introduces dangerous circularity. The probe would learn the generative model's internal stereotype of an era rather than the actual historical reality. Therefore, anchoring against codec-resynthesized real references remains the optimal and most scientifically rigorous approach.

## **3\. Content and Melody Preservation**

A successful era-targeted cover must impose a new stylistic wrapper without corrupting the underlying compositional identity of the source track. The preservation of melody, harmony, rhythm, and large-scale structure must be evaluated through metrics strictly invariant to timbral and stylistic shifts.

### **Pitch and Chroma Similarity**

The standard approach to measuring harmonic preservation, as utilized in the evaluation of MusicGen-Melody, MuseControlLite, and MusiConGen, relies on chromagram extraction and alignment31. A chromagram collapses the frequency spectrum into a 12-dimensional pitch-class profile, theoretically ignoring timbre while capturing the harmonic progression. To account for slight temporal deviations generated by the model, Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) computes the optimal monotonic alignment between the original and generated chromagrams, returning the mean cosine similarity along the warping path33.  
However, Chroma DTW exhibits a notoriously high baseline floor. Because Western popular music relies heavily on shared diatonic scales and common chord progressions, pairing two entirely different songs in the same key can yield Chroma DTW scores between 0.78 and 0.8833. Consequently, Chroma DTW is insufficient for proving strict melodic preservation. To achieve granular melodic verification, pitch extraction models such as RMVPE (Robust Multi-view Pitch Extraction) or CREPE are required36. By extracting the raw fundamental frequency (![][image2]) contour from the vocal or lead melody line, the Pearson correlation or raw pitch accuracy between the original and generated contours provides a highly sensitive, un-gameable metric for melodic fidelity34.

### **Cover Song Identification (CSI) Distance**

Cover Song Identification models represent the state of the art in content preservation measurement. These deep learning architectures are explicitly trained via contrastive loss to map varied renditions of a single composition into a tight cluster within a latent embedding space, actively ignoring immense variations in instrumentation, tempo, language, and era style.  
The CoverHunter architecture provides an exceptionally robust backbone for this task. It utilizes a Conformer network—merging convolutional layers for local acoustic feature extraction with transformer layers for global temporal context—coupled with an attention-based time-domain pooling module38. CoverHunter is trained using a coarse-to-fine chunk alignment loss that makes it highly resilient to structural rearrangements38. It outputs a dense 128- or 256-dimensional embedding38. By computing the cosine distance between the CoverHunter embeddings of the original mix and the generated cover, preservation can be quantified independent of the era shift. Alternative CSI frameworks, such as the ByteCover family or CLEWS, similarly utilize multi-loss training to construct invariant compositional representations42.  
To contextualize CSI distances, the metric must be calibrated. The distance between the original and the cover must be benchmarked against the average true-positive distance (known covers) and true-negative distance (unrelated songs) derived from standard CSI datasets such as SHS100K or DaTacos38.

### **Structural and Rhythmic Agreement**

Preservation extends beyond melody into temporal and formal structure. Using the Music Structure Analysis Framework (MSAF) and the mir\_eval library, higher-order musical geometry can be quantified48. The Structural Pairwise F-measure evaluates the retention of broad segmentation boundaries (e.g., verse-chorus transitions), while the Beat F-measure and Delta BPM metrics assess the stability of the rhythmic grid and tempo32. A successful generation should exhibit a Beat F-measure closely approaching 1.0, indicating that the generative model did not hallucinate extra beats or drift out of time with the original vocal stem33.

## **4\. Audio Quality and Prompt Adherence**

In the context of ultra-small datasets, evaluating the absolute audio quality of generative outputs requires metrics that decouple acoustic fidelity from statistical distribution requirements.

### **Neural Audio Quality Predictors**

Traditional generative metrics like FAD compute distances between distributions, necessitating large populations to form stable manifolds. When evaluating twelve tracks across multiple seeds, neural quality predictors trained via human-annotation distillation offer a superior paradigm. The Meta Audiobox Aesthetics model represents the current frontier in this domain53. Built upon a massive 768-dimensional WavLM transformer backbone, Audiobox Aesthetics directly regresses utterance-level quality scores across independent axes without requiring any reference audio53.  
The model outputs granular predictions for Production Quality (PQ) and Content Enjoyment (CE)53. The PQ axis evaluates technical fidelity, targeting clarity, dynamic range, spatialization, and the absence of codec artifacts, while the CE axis predicts subjective artistic expression and emotional impact53. Because Audiobox Aesthetics is a deterministic forward-pass neural predictor, it provides highly stable, sample-independent quality measurements perfect for small-scale ablation studies.

### **Secondary Quality Checks**

While Audiobox Aesthetics handles perceptual quality, KAD (via the kadtk toolkit) and FAD\-![][image1] should be employed as secondary checks to measure distributional deviation from the training corpus9. For prompt adherence, the CLAP score remains the standard metric for verifying that the generator acknowledged the text conditioning19. However, as previously noted, CLAP scores must be treated as secondary verifications rather than primary optimization targets to prevent the degradation of acoustic quality through over-optimization23.

## **5\. Vocal and Remix Consistency**

The specified generative pipeline preserves the original vocal stem, generating a novel era-specific accompaniment that is subsequently remixed. This hybrid approach introduces the risk of profound musical and acoustic dissonance between the fixed human performance and the synthetic instrumental.

### **Mix Balance and Spectral Masking**

A successful cover requires professional mix translation. The relative energy balance between the vocal and the accompaniment must be quantified to ensure the generative model has not produced a wall of sound that drowns out the vocal. Utilizing the pyloudnorm library, the integrated loudness of both the original vocal stem and the generated accompaniment can be calculated in LUFS according to the EBU R128 standard27. The Vocal-to-Accompaniment Ratio (VAR) establishes whether the generative output adheres to traditional mixing paradigms (where the vocal typically sits slightly above the instrumental bed).  
Furthermore, the spectral masking induced by the new accompaniment must be measured. A novel proxy for this involves re-separating the final remix using a state-of-the-art source separation model, such as HT Demucs4. By passing the generated mix through Demucs and extracting the isolated vocal, the Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR) between the originally injected vocal and the newly re-extracted vocal can be computed4. If the generated accompaniment aggressively masks the vocal frequencies, the Demucs algorithm will struggle to cleanly isolate the vocal, resulting in severe extraction artifacts and a correspondingly low SI-SDR score.

### **Harmonic and Rhythmic Symbiosis**

To ensure the generated accompaniment remains musically coherent with the vocal, continuous pitch and tempo verification is required. Applying ![][image2] extraction to both stems and computing the cross-correlation of their chromagrams over time ensures that the generative model successfully followed the harmonic constraints of the melody. Additionally, calculating the localized phase offset between the rhythmic transients of the vocal and the accompaniment verifies that the generated track has not suffered from temporal drift, ensuring strict rhythmic symbiosis.

## **6\. Information-Theoretic Probing and Analysis Metrics**

The analytical half of the project seeks to attribute release decade and market information to specific isolated stems (vocals versus accompaniment) using embeddings from self-supervised models like MERT and MuQ29. MERT leverages a dual-teacher framework combining EnCodec acoustic tokens with CQT musical tokens, while MuQ employs Mel-Residual Vector Quantization to capture deep structural representations30. However, utilizing raw linear probe accuracy to determine the presence of era information in these embeddings is highly flawed. A highly parameterized probe might achieve high accuracy by memorizing dataset-specific artifacts, track identities, or confounding variables (e.g., artist overlap) rather than extracting genuine stylistic geometry29.

### **Selectivity and Minimum Description Length**

To rigorously attribute information to the embeddings, control tasks must be introduced. In a control task, the dataset is duplicated, and random era labels are deterministically assigned to each track67. The linear probe is trained to predict both the true era labels and the random control labels. The ultimate metric of representational quality is Selectivity, defined as the difference between the accuracy on the true task and the accuracy on the control task67. High Selectivity mathematically proves that the probe is utilizing the structured geometry of the MuQ or MERT latent space to make decisions, rather than relying on its own capacity to memorize arbitrary mappings.  
Selectivity can be further refined through Information-Theoretic Probing via the Minimum Description Length (MDL)66. MDL probing, implemented via online coding or variational coding, evaluates the amount of theoretical "effort" required by the probe to transmit the labels given the representations66. If the era information is cleanly and linearly separable within the stem's embedding, the description length will be minimal. By comparing the MDL of the vocal stem against the accompaniment stem, researchers can precisely quantify which component natively houses more chronological information without confounding the result with probe capacity69.

### **Ordinal Metrics for Chronological Data**

Decade classification (1960s to 2010s) is an ordinal problem, not a categorical one. A standard cross-entropy loss function incorrectly treats a misclassification of a 1960s track as a 1970s track with the same severity as misclassifying it as a 2010s track. This destroys the chronological topology of the evaluation.  
Therefore, the primary metric for decade attribution must be the Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)72. QWK applies a penalty matrix to the classification confusion matrix, where the penalty grows quadratically with the distance from the true era label75. Utilizing QWK, alongside Mean Absolute Error (MAE) for decade distance, provides a mathematically sound reflection of how well the embeddings organize temporal data75.  
To compute the significance of stem-wise attribution (e.g., proving the accompaniment holds more era information than the vocals), the QWK of a probe trained on full mixes is compared against ablated probes. Given the small validation set (\~132 clips), exact McNemar tests for paired categorical data or bootstrapping the QWK scores across tracks provides robust 95% confidence intervals, enabling statistically valid effect size reporting29.

## **7\. Metric Validation Framework**

Before any quantitative metric is deployed on the generated audio, it must undergo a rigorous, automated meta-evaluation to guarantee its reliability without requiring human listening panels. This framework establishes acceptance criteria for the chosen metrics.

### **Automated Stress-Testing**

> 1. **Positive Controls (Anchored Baselines):** The metrics must successfully evaluate real tracks of a known era. By passing a 1980s validation set through the EnCodec module, the resulting audio represents a "perfect" generation (flawless composition with generative artifacts). A valid metric (e.g., KAD or resynthesized probing) must consistently identify this control as the 1980s, proving it can see past the domain shift28.  
> 2. **Negative Controls (Compositional Destruction):** To validate the CoverHunter content preservation metric, deliberate mismatches are constructed. The original vocal stem is paired with an accompaniment track randomly shuffled from an entirely different song or era. A valid CSI metric must exhibit a catastrophic drop in cosine similarity, confirming it measures true harmonic and melodic alignment rather than superficial audio quality38.  
> 3. **Algorithmic Sensitivity Curves:** Using libraries such as pedalboard13, progressive degrees of digital signal processing (e.g., severe low-pass filtering, overdrive, pitch shifting) are applied to the validation audio. Robust metrics like Audiobox Aesthetics and FAD\-![][image1] must demonstrate monotonic degradation corresponding to the intensity of the DSP application, mapping a clean sensitivity curve13.  
> 4. **Seed-to-Seed Stability:** Generative models utilize stochastic sampling. By generating two seeds for every prompt and track, the intra-song variance of the metric can be quantified. If a metric fluctuates wildly across seeds of the same prompt, it lacks the stability required for small-sample analysis.

A metric is deemed acceptable for the final suite only if it perfectly separates positive from negative controls (![][image3]), exhibits monotonic sensitivity to degradation, and demonstrates a Selectivity score significantly greater than zero during probing tasks67.

## **8\. Statistical Inference for Small Sample Sizes**

The evaluation dataset represents an extreme low-sample constraint: 12 songs ![][image4] 3 target eras ![][image4] 2 seeds yields only 72 generated clips. In this hierarchical structure, the observations are not independent. Treating the 72 clips as independent data points constitutes pseudo-replication, severely inflating the risk of Type I errors (false positives). Standard aggregation followed by non-parametric tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) collapses the variance and masks the underlying distributions.

### **Power Analysis and Multiple Comparisons**

Evaluating three separate era conditions requires post-hoc pairwise comparisons. To prevent the inflation of family-wise error rates, False Discovery Rate (FDR) adjustments (such as the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) must be applied to the LMM p-values.

To bolster confidence in the reported effect sizes, bootstrapping over the 12 songs (resampling with replacement) can be utilized to generate robust 95% confidence intervals for the marginal means extracted from the LMM29.

## **Unverified or Uncertain**

While the proposed framework establishes rigorous controls, certain computational paradigms remain unverified in the broader literature. A deterministic, widely accepted "vocal-accompaniment symbiosis" metric that measures precisely how well an accompaniment *stylistically and musically* fits a fixed vocal stem—beyond raw pitch/chroma correlation and masking measurements—does not currently exist as a unified standard. Additionally, the temporal precision of CLAP embeddings is highly uncertain; it is unverified whether LAION-CLAP genuinely models the granular historical nuances between "1960s" and "1980s" prompts, or if it merely detects broad "retro" semantic approximations. Therefore, CLAP must not be utilized as a primary optimization objective for chronological accuracy.
